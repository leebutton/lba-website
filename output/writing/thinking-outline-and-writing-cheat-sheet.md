# Part 1: The opening article

## Title

**Delivering healthcare, creating health**

## Its place in Thinking

This is the opening point-of-view article. It connects my practical consultancy work to the wider ambition of Creating Health.

It can be published as an article while the Creating Health conversations remain coming soon.

## Central argument

Financial and organisational discipline matters, but it is too narrow a philosophy for neighbourhood working. Our starting point shapes whose priorities count, what we build, and what we recognise as progress.

## Suggested standfirst

*Draft for my review:*

> Neighbourhood working needs financial and organisational discipline. It also needs a wider starting point: the people, relationships, and conditions through which health is created.

## The outline

### 1. The language sets the direction

Keep the opening challenge to business language, including the acknowledgement that finance and governance matter.

Explain how organisational priorities can become the whole purpose of neighbourhood working. Make the tension clear without dismissing the responsibilities readers carry.

### 2. Start with everyday life

Bring together the conditions in the original post: trust, belonging, housing, work, relationships, access, contribution, safety, and community.

Introduce Wendell Berry's influence. The phrase “smallest unit of health” comes from *Health is Membership*. His understanding of community includes place and its creatures, a broader meaning than a service geography.

Use this as an intellectual influence on the argument, not as empirical proof.

### 3. More than services in a smaller geography

Keep the distinction between reorganising services and creating health.

Retain the questions about whether people likely to be marginalised are:

- better reached;
- better understood;
- better supported; and
- more able to shape the conditions affecting their health.

Explore what service activity figures can show, and what else leaders need to understand.

### 4. Communities with the ability to act

Develop what changes when people help determine the starting point.

Use Cormac Russell's influence to explore communities as contributors to health and citizen-led action. Retain the point that statutory services are necessary but insufficient.

Include a clear boundary: recognising community agency does not mean transferring statutory responsibilities to communities or expecting unpaid effort to compensate for missing resources.

Consider whose voice is heard and whose circumstances make participation difficult.

### 5. A discipline suited to complex work

Keep Cynefin as a short supporting passage, preserving the original honesty that I am exploring it as I develop my practice.

Describe bounded, safe-to-fail probes, observation, and adaptation in the complex parts of neighbourhood working. Avoid implying that every part of healthcare requires the same approach.

Explain the practical implication in plain language. The article does not need to become a framework explainer.

### 6. What changes in practice?

Add one genuine example or a few questions leaders could use.

A possible example to recall: when did an organisational question narrow a discussion, and what became possible when someone asked a different question?

An anonymised example is enough. If no suitable example comes to mind, use a question or reasoned explanation. Do not invent an anecdote.

Retain the closing line exactly:

> Healthcare is delivered. Health is created. We should not confuse the two.

## The remaining writing work

- Add one concrete moment or practical prompt.
- Add the boundary about statutory responsibilities and community resources.
- Consider “we risk reaching first for business-like solutions” in place of “we will opt to use business-like solutions”. This preserves the challenge without making the relationship inevitable.
- Check that the acknowledgement of healthcare's contribution to health remains clear before the rhetorical closing contrast.
- Add the source links below where they support the argument.

The LinkedIn post is already a substantive draft. Edit for usefulness; do not expand it merely to reach a word count. Approximately 800–1,000 words is an optional guide, not a target.

# Part 2: My writing cheat sheet

## The commitment

For the next four weeks, I am testing a routine I can repeat.

**Two appointments a week. One working draft at a time.**

- Drafting appointment: 25 minutes.
- Revision appointment: 25 minutes, on a different day.
- Minimum session when the week is difficult: 10 minutes.

My drafting appointment: ______________________________

My revision appointment: ______________________________

Attach each to an existing point in the week: after a regular meeting, after breakfast, or before opening email. Choose times I can realistically protect.

Success is keeping the appointments and moving a piece forward. Publication follows when it is ready; I do not need a public weekly promise.

## Capture: two minutes when something catches my attention

Keep one running note called **Things worth writing about**.

Capture three lines:

1. What happened, or what did I notice?
2. Why did it stay with me?
3. What question does it raise?

Use enough detail to remember the thought. Keep confidential names and identifying details out of the capture note.

Capture an idea without turning it into a writing obligation. The list is a source of material, not a backlog I owe anyone.

## Draft: 25 minutes

**Minutes 0–3: choose one reader and one point.**

Complete:

> I am writing for someone who __________.
>
> The thing I want them to see is __________.

**Minutes 3–20: write the rough version.**

Start wherever the thought is clearest. An opening sentence I can use:

> What I keep noticing is...

Follow the thought through:

- Here is the situation.
- Here is what I think is happening.
- Here is why that matters.
- Here is what someone might ask or do differently.

Use my ordinary speaking voice. Leave `[check]`, `[source]`, or `[example]` where something is missing. Keep the browser closed while drafting unless the source is essential to the point.

**Minutes 20–25: make the next session easy.**

Read once. Mark the sentence that contains the real argument. Write a handover note:

> Next time, I need to __________.

Stop without requiring the piece to be finished.

## Revise: 25 minutes on another day

Read it aloud, then work through these questions:

1. Can I state the point in one sentence?
2. Does the reader reach that point soon enough?
3. Where have I used an abstraction when something concrete would be clearer?
4. What would a thoughtful reader disagree with?
5. Which claims need evidence, qualification, or removal?
6. What can the reader take into their own work?

Cut repetition. Remove sentences whose main job is to sound impressive. Resolve the important placeholders. Keep the phrases that sound like me.

Finish by choosing one next action: another revision, a source check, outside feedback, or publication preparation.

## A field note is enough

A field note can be 300–500 words about one observation:

> **I noticed this.**
>
> **I think it matters because...**
>
> **Here is a question or action worth trying.**

It does not need a named framework, a dramatic anecdote, an image, or a comprehensive answer. A question is enough when I explain why it matters.

## When I get stuck

| If I notice... | My next move |
| --- | --- |
| “I need a better idea.” | Open the capture note and choose something I can describe specifically. |
| “I need to read more first.” | Write what I currently think, mark the uncertain claims, and research those afterwards. |
| “The opening is wrong.” | Start with the example or the sentence I most want to say. Return to the opening later. |
| “This is obvious.” | Identify the reader or situation for which it might be useful. If there is no useful point, put it aside. |
| “This is becoming three articles.” | Keep one argument. Move the other ideas into the capture note. |
| “I don't have 25 minutes.” | Write for 10 minutes and leave a next-step note. |
| “I missed a session.” | Resume at the next appointment. No catch-up debt. |
| “I keep polishing it.” | Use the ready-to-share check below and ask for one focused review. |

## Ready to share

- [ ] There is one clear point.
- [ ] The experience and judgement are mine.
- [ ] Facts and quotations are checked; uncertainty is honestly expressed.
- [ ] Examples are safe to share and permissions are resolved where needed.
- [ ] The piece gives the reader something useful.
- [ ] I have read it aloud and corrected distracting errors.

It can be ready while still leaving room for disagreement and further thought.

## How I can use AI after writing

Paste this request with my draft:

> Act as a critical reader. Do not rewrite this or generate replacement paragraphs. First, tell me what you think my central argument is. Then identify up to three places where the reasoning is unclear, too broad, unsupported, or repetitive. Give me the strongest reasonable objection and flag claims or quotations that need checking. Point to the relevant passages and ask questions that help me revise them myself. Preserve my voice. If the draft already works, say so; do not invent problems to fill the list.

For factual verification, add:

> Check the flagged claims against primary sources. Distinguish factual errors from differences of interpretation, and give me the sources so I can make the edit.
